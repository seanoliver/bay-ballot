Blueprint SF's Voter Guide
San Francisco is on an upswing. But in order to keep it that way, we need to remain informed, engaged voters. Good governance, transit, and tax reform plus important elected positions are on the ballot this November, and Blueprint has recommendations for voters who are invested in helping San Francisco thrive.
Why should you care about what we think? Blueprint SF cares about what works, not what sounds good. Formerly TogetherSF, we're the political home for San Franciscans who love this city and want our elected leaders to focus on results, not ideology. That means better public safety, better public education, clean streets, accountable government, and a thriving local economy. If you care about pragmatic and competent local government, we are here for you.

Board of Education
SFUSD is turning the corner - after years of mis-management, the school district has made real progress towards recovery. However, with three seats up for election this upcoming November, the future of SFUSD is on the ballot. Voters this November will decide whether or not SFUSD continues its work addressing the district's budget deficit and improving student outcomes or if it falls back into dysfunction. That’s why it’s incredibly important we elect the following candidates for the Board of Education.
Phil Kim:
SFUSD is in a fragile recovery. Under Kim's leadership as Board President, SFUSD’s structural deficit has shrunk and enrollment is showing positive signs of recovery. Additionally, he’s well credentialed. He's a former 7th grade science teacher who spent years developing science curriculum. His steady leadership and qualifications have won him the endorsements of Mayor Daniel Lurie, the SF Democratic Party, SF Parents Action, and others. In the previous June special election, residents overwhelmingly voted to keep Kim on the School Board. Let’s make sure he can keep up the good work and re-elect him this November. Having a steady hand on the Board of Education is critical for SFUSD’s recovery.
Autumn Brown Garibay
Autumn Brown Garibay is an SFUSD parent and PTA leader with a long history of advocating for students and parents. When she was a member of the SF Parents Leadership Council, she was heavily involved in promoting better educational outcomes for students, participating in SF Parents Kids Can’t Wait campaign and raising awareness about unequal academic results using hard data. She has a record of engaging with various stakeholders across the city, bringing people together, and standing up for students and parents in front of the SF Board of Education. She has demonstrated extensive knowledge of the challenges facing SFUSD such as the budget crisis, and shown a willingness to take them head on instead of kicking the can down the road. Now she is running for the Board of Education to give families a direct seat at the table. In addition to her advocacy work, Garibay brings private sector acumen to the position, currently working in a senior customer success role involved with strategy. She is endorsed by Mayor Daniel Lurie, the SF Democratic Party, SF Women’s Political Committee, SF Parents Action, and more. Vote for Autumn Brown Garibay to ensure families have a strong voice on the School Board.
Tim Tung
While much-needed progress has been made, SFUSD still faces a long-term budget deficit. A financial expert on the Board of Education would help SFUSD right the ship. That’s where Tim Tung comes in. Tung is a finance professional with decades of experience and the credentials to match. A Chartered Financial Analyst (CFA), Tung’s experience includes serving as the Acting Debt Administrator for the City of San Jose for four years and working as a Director for major credit rating firm, S&P Global, for more than decade. Today, he works for a major asset management firm, Allspring Global Investments. In addition to his professional work, Tung has been a member of San Francisco’s Citizens’ General Obligation Bond Oversight Committee, which is responsible for ensuring the City’s bond dollars are responsibly spent, since 2023. Tung’s competence and commitment earned him Mayor Daniel Lurie’s re-appointment to the committee in 2025. Demonstrating his fellow committee members’ confidence in him, Tung is currently the committee’s Chair. He is endorsed by Mayor Daniel Lurie, the SF Democratic Party, United Democratic Club, labor unions, and more. Vote for Tim Tung to set SFUSD on the right financial path.

Board of Supervisors
Under Mayor Daniel Lurie and the pragmatic majority on the Board of Supervisors, San Francisco as a whole has been on the path towards recovery. Crime is trending down, the number of homelessness encampments is down, tourism is coming back, and the economy is rebounding. Yet this progress is fragile, and electing the wrong leaders could set us back. That’s why we recommend voting for the following, solutions-focused leaders for the upcoming Board of Supervisors elections.
Stephen Sherrill for District 2 Supervisor
Since becoming supervisor back in 2024, Sherrill has supported stronger enforcement against drug dealing and helped pass an extension of SF’s First Year Free program, which waives certain fees for small businesses. Sherrill comes to the job with a data-driven mindset; he’s a former staffer for NYC Mayor Michael Bloomberg and prior to becoming supervisor led the Mayor’s Office of Innovation under former Mayor London Breed. Sherrill’s performance has won him widespread support in District 2 - in the previous June special election, he won 69% of the vote.
Alan Wong for District 4 Supervisor
Since becoming supervisor in 2025, Wong has supported policies necessary for San Francisco’s recovery. Wong supports fully staffing SFPD and making it easier for small businesses to open and operate in the city. Wong is also experienced: he is a life-long Sunset resident, two-term City College Board of Trustees President, former legislative aide, former policy director for Children’s Council of SF, and a California Army National Guard member. And D4 residents widely approve of the job Wong is doing so far - in the previous June special election, Wong won over 60% of the vote.
Matt Dorsey for District 6 Supervisor
Since joining the Board in 2022, Dorsey has been one of City Hall's most consistent voices for public safety and a recovery-first approach to the drug crisis. He has championed efforts to improve public safety, including addressing SFPD’s staffing shortage and supporting ’s Prop E, which allowed SFPD to leverage technology to pursue criminals. As the only board member who’s experienced recovery, he authored the Recovery First ordinance, passed unanimously and signed by Mayor Lurie in , making long-term remission the city's stated drug-policy goal. He also helped set up the RESET sobering center, which serves as an alternative to jail for those arrested for public substance use and tries to pivot them to long-term treatment and recovery. If you care about public safety and addressing SF’s drug crisis, re-elect Matt Dorsey for D6 this November.
Theo Ellington for District 10 Supervisor
Theo Ellington is running for District 10 to bring the Bayview and the city's southeast the same focus, urgency, and investment the rest of San Francisco takes for granted. Ellington’s platform includes investing in commercial corridors and bringing jobs to D10, ensuring the district has safe and clean streets, and supporting the community’s art and culture scene. Ellington’s background makes him well-equipped to take on these priorities.
Ellington is a third-generation San Franciscan and Bayview native - the first in his family to graduate college. He is now raising three young sons with his wife, a UCSF nurse, in the district. Ellington has both private and public sector experience and has a track record of delivering results. As the Golden State Warriors' first public affairs director, he helped bring the Chase Center to SF, boosting the City’s economy and creating thousands of jobs. He then later served as Salvation Army San Francisco’s Director of Homeless Initiatives and Community Development, spearheading the organization’s Covid-19 response and launching the successful recovery-focused The Way Out Program, which helps those in need get off the streets and into treatment. Additionally, he’s served multiple City commissions under different Mayors, including the Commission on Community Investment and Infrastructure. During his tenure on that commission, Ellington promoted projects in the Bayview Hunter’s Point, Mission Bay, and Transbay neighborhoods to build more housing.
Today, in addition to his day job working in public affairs, Ellington serves as the Executive Director of the Ruth Williams Opera House (formerly Bayview Opera House), a cultural landmark in District 10 that has flourished under his leadership. Ellington’s experience and community connections has earned him a wide array of endorsements including Mayor Daniel Lurie, Lieutenant Governor Eleni Kounalakis, Congresswoman Lateefah Simon, Assemblywoman Catherine Stefani, D6 Supervisor Matt Dorsey, SF Democratic Party Vice Chair Cedric Akbar, and more. A vote for Theo Ellington is a vote for a revitalized District 10.
Ballot Measures
Good governance, transit, tax reform and other measures are on this November’s ballot. Here are our recommendations.
Prop A - Charter Changes Affecting Various City Departments and Commissions
This charter amendment eliminates redundant or unnecessary commissions (like the Sanitation and Streets Commission), requires regular convening of a Commission Streamlining Task Force every 10-years to review the commission system, and removes outdated legal language.
We recommend voting Yes on A. This is a common-sense measure that streamlines city government. Reforming the commission structure will reduce redundant bureaucracy and create a more efficient and responsive government.
Prop B - Establishing a Municipal Finance Corporation and a Public Bank
This charter amendment would authorize the establishment of a Municipal Finance Corporation (MFC) and a Public Bank as non-profit corporations, subject to the availability of adequate funding and resources. It would also set forth the mission, principles and governance structures of those organizations, including authorizing the creation of an MFC Oversight Commission, Bank Oversight Commission, MFC Board of Directors and Bank Board of Directors.
We recommend voting No on B. There's good reason to be skeptical that a public bank would be well-run in SF, especially given that the city's current deficit stems from overspending. Financial experts have also warned that the conditions that make the Bank of North Dakota successful (a state economy that benefited from a fracking boom) don't exist in San Francisco, making the comparison a weak basis for the proposal.
Prop C - Contributions to the Housing Fund
This charter amendment renews and expands the Housing Trust Fund, which exists to support affordable housing in the city. It would raise the annual city government contribution to over $125M and extend the program through 2058. It doesn't create a new tax or raise tax rates.
We are neutral on C. There's no strong case for either supporting or opposing this one. It doesn't add new tax burdens, and the deficit-triggered freeze provision is a reasonably fiscally responsible safeguard. However, this measure doesn’t address the fundamental issue that the cost for building an affordable housing unit in SF is still too high (around $900,000 per some estimates).
Prop D - Changes to Ballot Measure Process
This charter amendment tightens San Francisco's lax rules for getting measures onto the ballot, bringing us in line with other California cities like San Diego and LA. This charter amendment would eliminate the Mayor’s unilateral ability to put a measure on the ballot. It would also eliminate the ability of four supervisors to put a measure on the ballot. The Board of Supervisors could still place measures on the ballot, but only with a six-vote majority. Additionally, the charter amendment would raise the signature threshold for citizen-led initiatives to 8% of registered voters and raise the signature threshold to call a special election on an initiative ordinance to 10% of registered voters. Finally, the charter amendment would allow backers of a signature initiative to withdraw their measure 102 days before the election.
We recommend voting Yes on D. We support this measure because San Francisco’s current ballot measure system is flawed and results in overly long ballots in which residents are asked to vote on a wide variety of measures that can be quite complex in nature. 2023’s Rose Report (commissioned by our predecessor organization, TogetherSF) and good government think tank SPUR have both highlighted how San Francisco requires a lower signature threshold for placing measures on the ballot versus other major California cities like San Diego and Los Angeles. These lax requirements lead to numerous problems: elected leaders deflecting from making tough decisions (sometimes on complex topics like taxes) and deferring them to voters, special interest groups placing misleading measures on ballot to support their own interests i.e. “CEO” tax, and electeds/groups using measures to wage political battles/confuse voters i.e. dueling measures like Prop D and E (both related to housing) or Prop D and E (both related to commission reform).
Prop E - City Administrator’s Authority and Changes to City Contracting
This charter amendment expands the City Administrator's authority over contracting, centralizing and streamlining a process that's currently scattered across individual city departments. The City Administrator would gain exclusive power to propose procurement ordinances to the Board of Supervisors; those ordinances would take effect after 60 days unless the Board or Mayor rejects them, and the Board could not amend them. Thresholds for Board approval of contracts would rise sharply - from $1 million to $4.5 million for revenue-generating contracts and leases, and from $10 million to $25 million for expenditure contracts - with the Controller adjusting both for inflation every five years starting in 2032. The Board would still review contract amendments that cumulatively raise a contract by 50% or more above the last Board-approved figure. The City Administrator could also issue procurement rules that override conflicting department rules and set citywide technology policy, with that authority extending to, with some extensions, the SFMTA, PUC, Airport, Port, and Charitable Trust Departments. Finally, the City Administrator's term would double from five to ten years.
We recommend voting Yes on E. This charter amendment makes critical reforms to our City government’s contracting system. Currently, the City’s contracting process is convoluted and confusing, leading to a lack of competitive bidding and higher costs for the City. For example, the notorious $1.7 million Noe Valley public toilet was a product of SF’s current contracting process. This measure would reform City contracting by giving the independent City Administrator the power to standardize and clarify purchasing rules across City departments while still maintaining important Board of Supervisors oversight. These changes will streamline our contracting process, reducing costs and enhance government efficiency.
Prop F - Changes to Executive Branch Management
This charter amendment would expand the Mayor’s ability to lead the City government’s executive branch. This charter amendment would give the Mayor the ability to directly hire and fire department heads, a power that mayors in other major cities like NYC and Boston already have. The Mayor would also gain the authority to hire deputy mayors. The charter amendment would also allow appointing authorities to directly appoint and remove their appointees on City commissions, with three exceptions: the Board of Appeals, Civil Service Commission, and Ethics Commission. Finally, under the charter amendment the Mayor could reorganize most Charter-created departments, transferring duties, merging departments under a single head, or reassigning commission oversight, and would also gain authority to hire deputy mayors. A long list of departments would be shielded from this reorganization authority however, including the City Administrator, Controller, Port, Airport, SFMTA, PUC, Department of Elections, Ethics Commission, and any department led by another elected official. Two carve-outs are explicit: the Department of Police Accountability could not be folded into the Police Department, and the Sheriff's Inspector General could not be folded into the Sheriff's Office.
We recommend voting Yes on F. This charter amendment makes much-needed changes to SF’s government structure as recommended both by 2023’s Rose Report (sponsored by our predecessor organization TogetherSF) and by good government think tank SPUR. This charter amendment would clarify lines of authority and thus accountability in government, making it clear that the elected Mayor is responsible for department performance by giving the Mayor direct hire/fire authority over department heads. Additionally, this charter amendment creates a clear system for holding un-elected commissioners by giving their appointing authorities (typically the elected Mayor or elected BOS) the ability to directly remove them. Finally, this measure gives the Mayor the flexibility to merge redundant City departments and agencies + hire deputy Mayors to help manage SF’s sprawling City government. Overall, this measure will create a more effective and accountable City Hall that will be better structured to address resident needs.
Prop G - Allowing Private Vehicles on the Great Highway in Sunset Dunes Park
The measure would allow private motor vehicles to use the Upper Great Highway in Sunset Dunes Park at all times, except for between Fridays at to Mondays at and on holidays. Emergency vehicles, official government vehicles, intra-park transit shuttle buses, and other similar authorized vehicles would continue to have access to the Upper Great Highway at all times.
We are neutral on G. There are good arguments on both sides here, and reasonable San Franciscans have landed in different places. But the Great Highway has now consumed multiple election cycles. We're taking no position on Prop G and encouraging voters to decide it themselves-then let the city turn its attention to the problems that affect all of us.
Prop H - Parcel Tax to Fund Public Muni Operations
This measure would create a new annual parcel tax dedicated to SFMTA transit operations, running from through , with annual inflation adjustments. Tax amounts scale with property type and building size. Landlords of rent-controlled units can pass through up to 50% of the tax, capped at $65 per unit, but only on tenancies whose base rent was set before . The measure is estimated to raise $177 million annually beginning in Fiscal Year 2027-2028, increasing over time as the parcel tax adjusts to inflation.
We recommend voting No on H. Transit is important. But so is everything the city provides -that doesn’t mean we keep charging taxpayers more instead of fixing the city budget. SF residents already pay some of the highest taxes in the country and our city has a high cost of living. When we’re in the middle of a cost of living crisis, the last thing our residents need is a new tax that will heavily impact homeowners and renters. Additionally, this tax measure disappointingly does not require SFMTA to make structural financial changes to help address its financial deficit.
Prop I - Changes to Real Property Transfer Tax
This measure restructures San Francisco's existing transfer tax on high-value property sales to create a permanent, dedicated funding stream for affordable housing. The mechanism is a split: the existing transfer tax rate would be cut in half on properties sold for $10 million or more, and a new "House SF Tax" would fill the gap at the same rate, meaning most sellers would pay the same total tax as today, but half would now be legally restricted to housing use. All “House SF Tax” revenue would flow into a dedicated fund with strict allocations, with the majority of revenue dedicated to affordable housing programs. The measure also restricts the Board of Supervisors' current ability to amend the underlying transfer tax law without voter approval, locking in both rates and dedicated uses until voters say otherwise.
We recommend voting No on I. This measure moves General Fund money into an earmarked fund, which means the city loses flexibility to use those dollars where they're needed most, like covering budget shortfalls. The city is already facing a General Fund budget deficit. This will only make it worse.
Prop J - Removal of Foreclosure Exemption for Real Property Transfer Tax
This measure would remove the existing real estate transfer tax partial exemption for properties wherein A) lender foreclosed on the property or B) borrowers transferred the property to lender in lieu of foreclosure. This measure would apply to transfers on or after . The Controller estimates this measure could bring an average of $100 million to $150 million annually in tax revenue over the next five-years.
We recommend voting No on J. Originally, this measure was supposed to be paired with a reduction in the real transfer tax to be part of a revenue-neutral package. But now that it’s not, all this measure does is raise taxes, hampering Downtown’s recovery. And by just raising revenue, this measure doesn't address the real cause of SF's deficit: overspending. SF doesn't have a revenue problem. It has a spending problem. Reaching for more revenue avoids tackling that fundamental issue.
