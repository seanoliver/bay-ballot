Endorsement: If you support the billionaire tax, vote no on Props. 41 and 42

Voters should be careful when they get to Propositions 41 and 42 on the November ballot. They are not what they seem. Read in isolation, they might sound like good ideas, but they are really poison pills, an electoral bulwark that billionaires hope will stop a tax on their wealth. We recommend voters say no to both.
Proposition 40, also on the ballot, would impose a one-time tax of 5% on the net worth of Californians worth more than $1 billion. Most of the money collected would fund health care to help offset federal Medicaid cuts that President Donald Trump signed into law last year. The Press Democrat editorial board has recommended a yes vote on Prop. 40.
Props. 41 and 42 would both amend the state constitution to stop Prop. 40. If either gets more yes votes than Prop. 40, it would prevent the billionaire tax from taking effect. Building a Better California, a group bankrolled by Google co-founder Sergey Brin and other billionaires, has raised more than $156 million to support Props. 41 and 42 and more than $88 million to defeat Prop. 40.
Curiously, a September Public Policy Institute of California poll found that all three could pass with slim majorities. If that happens, it would come down to whether Prop. 40 gets the most votes. Californians who vote yes on Prop. 40 should vote no on Props. 41 and 42. If they do not, they vote against themselves. Californians who oppose the billionaire tax, on the other hand, should ask whether it is wise to permanently amend the constitution with flawed measures to stop one tax.
Proposition 41
Prop. 41 claims to be an accountability measure that would require better auditing of new special taxes and compliance with the state’s voter-approved spending limit. California has plenty of cases of spending racing out of control without much accountability, but Prop. 41 uses that as a diversion.
One problem is that the audits would begin before an initiative even qualifies for the ballot. That would increase costs for the state auditor, which means it would increase costs for taxpayers. If this were a straightforward measure to require better auditing, it would have our support. It is not.
Another provision explains why Prop. 41 is on the ballot. It would nullify any state taxes enacted after , that exempt their revenue from the state spending limit. Taxes like, say, Prop. 40’s billionaire tax.
There is an interesting case to make that new taxes should fit within the spending limit, but retroactively creating that system to target one tax on about 200 billionaires is not the right way to get there.
Proposition 42
Prop. 42 is more direct but no less harmful. It would ban any new state tax on personal property enacted after . Taxes like, say, Prop. 40’s billionaire tax. It would also prohibit taxes that apply retroactively. Not coincidentally, Prop. 40 applies to billionaires who lived in California on .
Again, there is an interesting case to make that retroactive taxes are bad, but putting a constitutional ban on the same ballot as one of those taxes is dirty pool.
The more troubling part of the measure is that it would take an entire class of taxes off the table for good. Voters who do not like the billionaire tax can simply vote against Prop. 40. They do not need to prevent all future voters and lawmakers from considering a tax on personal property to deal with the challenges of a different time.
