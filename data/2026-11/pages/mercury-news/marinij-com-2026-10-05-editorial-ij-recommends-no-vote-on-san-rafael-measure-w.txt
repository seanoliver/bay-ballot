Endorsement: IJ recommends no vote on San Rafael Measure W

By Marin IJ Editorial Board
PUBLISHED: at PDT
Getting your Trinity Audio player ready...
In Measure W, the San Rafael City Council is asking voters to impose a tax most of them won’t have to pay.
The council has been moving forward with increasing other charges as a way to meet its budgetary obligations, but unlike fees, Measure W needs to go to the voters for their approval.
It is a hefty tax – a $10,000 tax on the sale of a $1 million home; a charge that local Realtors warn will cool the local real estate market, reduce the amount sellers will get for their houses and make it that much harder for people to afford to buy in San Rafael.
The city currently charges 0.2% of the sales price. Measure W would raise that charge by five-fold to 1% – highest in the county. That’s on top of the 0.11% the county charges. That’s a huge tax bite.
Supporters say it is a modest charge given the amount involved in local real estate sales. Mayor Kate Colin says Measure W is about maintaining the quality of life in San Rafael.
The increase, they say, is needed to raise an estimated $6 million per year, money they say will go to pay for maintaining emergency services to meet growing demand, fixing streets and sidewalks, addressing homelessness and for storm drains and flood control – all worthwhile responsibilities.
In recent years, San Rafael voters approved sales tax increases and bond measures to keep the budget balanced and to build and repair firehouses and a new library. Those taxes are being paid for by people who shop in San Rafael or those who own property – far more than would be paying this tax. Those are paid for by almost every resident, but the Measure W tax will be paid by the roughly 700 sellers or buyers.
The city faces a budget gap. For instance, it says it needs the tax revenue to continue to pay for its SAFE Team, the contracted squad of trained mental health professionals who respond to emergency calls for people – often homeless – experiencing a mental health crisis. It is a good program that makes sense.
Running a city requires the balance of a budget that reflects local priorities. SAFE should be among them. But, in Measure W, the city council is sending the message that it depends on voters passing the tax.
San Rafael’s financial quandary, officials contend, dates back to the 1978 passage of Proposition 13, the landmark property tax reform measure. Because San Rafael was the county’s economic hub at the time, most of the city’s budget was covered by local sales tax revenue, enabling the city to keep a lid on its property tax rate. Prop. 13 locked that lower rate. Over the years, the construction of other regional shopping centers cut into revenue growth. Add to that increasing city salaries, pension and medical benefits, as well as other costs that were unexpected – like spending millions to respond to homelessness.
Measure W has drawn stiff opposition from the Marin Board of Realtors, which is an important stakeholder in San Rafael. City officials – especially members of the city council – should have conferred with the group before the measure got to the agenda ahead of the vote to put it on the ballot.
That would have been politically wise and respectful due diligence.

Councilmember Maribeth Bushey maintains that the Measure W tax would not be a big part of any real estate transaction, but the board objected to the proposed tax increase and is actively opposing Measure W, contending it will chill local real estate sales.
City officials say the board’s contention is overblown and point to home sales in Albany as an example where sales have kept pace even since city voters passed the city’s 1.5% transfer tax.
The local real estate board’s warning is concerning. They know the local market. Also, the city benefits from the turnover of property, as the tax basis is typically increased with every sale.
On the ballot, San Rafael voters will also be asked to approve two more taxes – a parcel tax for childcare and another in support of MarinHealth Medical Center.
Affordability is a big concern. The big increase will bring in more than the city needs right now. In this economy, that’s not right. Officials should consider a smaller increase with less chance of impacting the real estate market.
