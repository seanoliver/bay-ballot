Endorsement: Marin childcare tax Measure P deserves support

Quality childcare and early education in Marin and the Bay Area have been unaffordable to many people for a long time. It’s especially brutal for lower earners in the workforce faced with the choice of being a parent at home or going out and earning money for the family.
Measure P, a proposed parcel tax on the ballot, would raise an estimated $12.5 million annually over 15 years to provide childcare scholarships to low- and middle-income children; boost the wages of childcare providers; and upgrade early care and education facilities.
The countywide ordinance would bolster existing infrastructure that distributes state and federal funding to licensed childcare and early education providers for children from 0 to 5 years old.
The argument from supporters is compelling. Childcare for families in need allows Marin workers to contribute to businesses that support the economy. That’s beneficial to all of us. Quality early education provides important cognitive and social skills during a time of life when 90% of the brain is developed.
“There are many benefits for children,” campaign representative Joanne Webster told the IJ editorial board. “From better education and health outcomes to a stronger economic outcome over their lifetime. This should be part of infrastructure that helps families and our economy thrive.”
Those in opposition to the measure agree that childcare and early education should be supported with public funding for Marin families who can’t afford it. They say concerns include the amount of money and the lack of detail in how it will be spent, as well as missing information on metrics for success. Those issues, detractors say, are enough to send Measure P back to the drawing board.
“A real problem does not justify a bad tax,” said Lucy Dilworth, a member of the Marin Coalition of Sensible Taxpayers Board of Directors, which opposes the measure in the voter guide.
“Voters do not have enough information to know what they are buying. The measure does not establish how the money will be divided.”
It is true that the language on the ballot and in the voter guide does not focus enough on how the pie will be sliced, but the full text of the ordinance details oversight and advisory committees, plus outlines the Marin County Board of Supervisors’ duty to commission an independent audit of all spending.
Supporters say any vagueness about how the money will be divided is meant to allow flexibility as the needs of families and the providers of early education and childcare change.
The editorial board agrees that details about spending, oversight and metrics for success could have been referenced on the ballot and definitely should have been outlined with more detail in the voter guide. But calls that the ordinance lacks specificity appear to show no faith in oversight committee members or elected supervisors who are specifically charged with ensuring taxpayer dollars are spent wisely.
The organizers have a detailed five-year plan to use the initial surge in funding to mold the structure of the expanded program. A second five-year term would allow stakeholders to implement findings in hopes of solidifying best practices and success before it is brought back to taxpayers ahead of Measure P’s 15-year sunset.
While the duration of the initial tax is longer than some, the structure appears to make sense as the county steps forward in hopes of firmly setting the parameters for a permanent program that actually works.
Doing so, as has been done in neighboring Sonoma and San Francisco counties, would be an important feather in Marin’s hat as it addresses the goal of better future outcomes for all Marin residents.
The ordinance also benefits families of workers who commute to Marin while living in more affordable parts of the Bay Area. The Marin economy relies on those often-essential workers. It makes sense to support the people who help keep our economy humming. Oversight watchdogs can and should make certain public dollars are truly for Marin workers providing services we demand.
Property owners would initially be required to pay a tax of 5 cents per building square foot on the first 100,000 square feet – amounting to about $100 a year for the average homeowner. Since the measure is on the ballot by voter petition, it needs a simple majority to pass. There are no exemptions available.
In support of lower-income families, today’s workforce and the positive outcomes we want for all Marin children as they grow, the IJ editorial board recommends a yes vote on Measure P in the November election.
