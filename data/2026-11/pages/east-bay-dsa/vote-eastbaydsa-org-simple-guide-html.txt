2026 East Bay DSA Voter Guide
Welcome to the East Bay DSA Voter Guide!
East Bay DSA has prepared the following analysis of ballot measures on the basis of our chapter’s Electoral Platform and DSA’s National Program. For information on state-wide ballot measures, please consult California DSA's voter guide.
How Do I Vote?
You can visit the California Secretary of State’s website to find your nearest polling location. Same day voter registration is available at county election offices and polling locations.
To vote by mail drop box, visit your county's website for your nearest location: Alameda County Contra Costa County Solano County Napa County
Ongoing efforts to restrict voting access and delay mail service may impact your ability to vote. In line with the Secretary of State’s guidance, if you are voting by mail, we strongly recommend completing and returning your ballot as soon as you receive it.
For information on your rights as a voter, we recommend referring to the ACLU of Northern California’s Know Your Rights Information.
Endorsements vs. Recommendations
Endorsements have been approved by our membership and entail a significant commitment of chapter resources, time, and energy. These are candidates and measures that advance our socialist demands. Endorsed measures and candidates are indicated with a 🌹.
Recommendations are not endorsements but are still valuable measures that can build working class power. Analysis is informed by our East Bay Electoral Platform and DSA's national program, “Workers Deserve More!”
For measures where we take No Position, we provide insight into how that measure could potentially benefit or hurt working class interests.
Analysis of statewide ballot measures and races is provided by California DSA.
East Bay DSA 2026 Electoral Platform
The East Bay DSA 2026 Electoral Platform is a core set of demands that are meant to unite our campaigns and committees in fighting for the working class. Its planks represent reforms that we can push for here and now as we organize to win our program for democratic socialist transformation. When our candidates enter office, the platform provides the basis on which we support their work and connect it to our organizing.

Nate Adams is the former Co-Chair of East Bay DSA and a community organizer. As an organizer for Richmond Progressive Alliance, he supported bringing people power and accountability to City Hall. He served on the national coordinating team for the Emergency Workplace Organizing Committee, which has supported grassroots labor power in thousands of workplaces. Adams’ roots in Oakland begin with his grandmother, who immigrated from Japan to Oakland. His public accountability platform focuses on corporate and public accountability, fiscal responsibility, and support for the Oakland People’s Arms Embargo.

Measure Z would impose a progressive (per square foot of building footprint) parcel tax to fund a public bank, which offers government agencies lower interest rates and fees for community-wide projects. As the loans are repaid, that money returns to the community through the form of new loans for more initiatives, instead of allowing private, for-profit banks to siphon away the money Over time, available funds for lending will grow exponentially to help sustain a local economy and fund projects that are consistent with our shared values and needs. .
Prop 3 - Renew State Income Tax Increase for Education Funding Initiative
Reviewed Copy link Print race
TL;DR: California DSA endorses a YES vote on Prop 3.
Recommendation Source: California DSA
Prop 3 makes permanent a wealth tax on the top 2% of income earners in the state in order to fund public education and healthcare (as of 2026, those making over $350,000). This wealth tax increases individual state income tax rates by 1% for those making over $370k and up to 3% for those making over $740k. This state income tax on high earners was initially passed in 2012 (as Prop 30) and extended in 2016 (as Prop 55).
If Prop 3 does not pass, the higher tax rates on the wealthy will sunset in 2031, and the state will see a loss of between $5-$15B in annual funding for public education. With the associated loss of funding for public education, 1 in 6 public school teachers will be facing layoffs. This is an important measure to maintain funding for public goods and a strong demonstration that taxing the wealthy works to advance the interests of the working class.
Prop 4 - Allow Public Financing of Election Campaigns Measure
Reviewed Copy link Print race
TL;DR: California DSA endorses a YES vote on Prop 4.
Recommendation Source: California DSA
Prop 4 repeals California's ban on state/local public campaign-finance systems and allows governments to establish programs for qualifying candidates who accept spending limits. It doesn't itself create a statewide public-financing program.
Electoral politics systematically privileges people who can raise money from wealthy donors, corporations, and professional political networks. In cities and states across the country, public financing of elections has allowed socialists and working people to compete on something at least marginally closer to a level playing field. Many socialist wins across the country, like Zohran Mamdani in New York City, would not have been possible without public financing. This is a clear positive step for working people, and can reduce candidates' dependence on wealthy private donors and allow working class mass membership organizations like our own to participate in larger and more expensive elections.
Prop 4 isn’t a silver bullet: it’s only enabling legislation, not the final reform. Winning Prop 4 creates space for socialists and reformers to fight locally/statewide over what strong public-finance systems actually look like. Public financing also does not eliminate capitalist political power, independent expenditures, lobbying, or wealthy ownership of media, or even come close to leveling the playing field. It is, however, an important and necessary step forward for working class political power.
Prop 40 - One-Time Wealth Tax for State-Funded Health Care Programs Initiative
Reviewed Copy link Print race
TL;DR: California DSA endorses a YES vote on Prop 40.
Recommendation Source: California DSA
Prop 40 would levy a one-time 5% wealth tax on individuals with over $1 billion in assets (a little over 220 people in California) in order to offset the loss of almost $100 billion in federal funding for Medi-Cal due to cuts from HR1 (the 'big beautiful bill'). In the absence of alternate funding, hospitals across the state will face closures, healthcare workers will be laid off, our most vulnerable neighbors will face increasing challenges to get the care they need, and mortality rates will rise.
Prop 40 responds to the immediate need for more healthcare funding, provides a corrective to Trump’s push for greater wealth consolidation, and offers a pilot wealth tax in order to address expanding inequality. Billionaires like Google founder Sergey Brin are spending hundreds of millions in an effort to fight this necessary redistribution of resources.
Measure RTM - Regional Transit Measure
Reviewed Copy link Print race
TL;DR: California DSA endorses a YES vote on Measure RTM, a regional transit measure on the ballot in Alameda and Contra Costa counties.
Recommendation Source: California DSA
California DSA has endorsed a Yes vote on the Bay Area Regional Transit Measure (Connect Bay Area) as a concrete fight against austerity and for a strong, publicly operated transit system. BART, Muni, AC Transit, and Caltrain collectively face operating deficits of more than $800 million annually starting in the 2027–28 fiscal year. The measure would raise approximately $980 million per year for 14 years to prevent major cuts to BART, Muni, AC Transit, Caltrain, and other Bay Area transit services, funded by a 0.5% sales tax in Alameda, Contra Costa, San Mateo, and Santa Clara counties and a 1% tax in San Francisco. It would also dedicate funding to reduced-fare and free transfers, expanded low-income fare programs, accessibility improvements, and projects that make transit faster and easier to use.
The sales-tax funding mechanism is regressive and falls short of the progressive taxation socialists should ultimately fight for. But the alternative is not a better funding system - it is deep service cuts that would certainly push Bay Area transit into a death spiral. Reduced frequency and shorter service hours would make transit less useful and reliable, causing ridership and fare revenue to fall, which would in turn create pressure for still more cuts. Once that cycle begins, rebuilding ridership and service becomes far more difficult and expensive. The burden would fall disproportionately on working-class people who depend on transit, while threatening thousands of unionized public-sector jobs and pushing more people toward expensive, car-dependent transportation.
Bay Area DSA members have already organized to secure regional transit funding, including Peninsula DSA’s successful campaign for San Mateo County to participate in the regional measure. DSA nationally has also identified accessible public transit and publicly owned transportation infrastructure as core socialist priorities.
A socialist vision is one with massively expanded public goods, and we strongly support rapid transit.

This is exactly the kind of general-election race where a socialist recommendation matters: voters have a genuine choice between an establishment liberal and a candidate rooted in the progressive and labor left.

Measure I - School Parcel Tax
Reviewed Copy link Print race
TL;DR: East Bay DSA recommends a YES vote on Measure I.
Measure I renews the expiring $96 school parcel tax, plus an additional $48 per parcel, for a total tax of $144 per parcel. This measure requires a ⅔ majority YES to pass. If approved, this parcel tax replaces Measure E, a parcel tax passed in 2019, which will expire on . If Measure I is not passed, Measure E will remain in effect until , at which point it will expire. Measure I is projected to raise approximately $2,900,000 ($2.9 million) annually for nine (9) years. It includes an exemption for senior-citizen homeowners, which is unfortunately not limited to lower-income residents. It also includes exemptions for people on SSI/SSDI (Supplemental Security Income/Social Security Disability Insurance), as well as independent oversight, a ban on using Measure I money to fund administrators’ salaries, and solely local control of funds. Measure I represents the first parcel tax increase since 2008.
Measure I seeks to provide local funding for Dublin schools that cannot be taken by the state, is locally controlled, and is subject to independent oversight. The goals of Measure I are to maintain small class sizes, attract and retain quality teachers, as well as provide enhanced STEM, reading, writing, visual and performing arts programs for students. Measure EE - Charter Amendment
Reviewed Copy link Print race
TL;DR: East Bay DSA issues no recommendation on Measure EE.
Measure EE would reform Oakland’s charter – the document that establishes Oakland’s system of government – to modify the powers of the mayor and city council. Measure EE establishes 9 major changes to how the city government works:
Define the mayor as the chief executive of the city
Empower the council to confirm the mayor’s appointees to directors of finance, human resources, public works, and transportation
Empower the council to create an independent budget and legislative analyst’s office to provide fiscal and policy analysis
Affirm councilmembers’ right to interface between constituents and city officials, and obligate city officials to be responsive
Empower the council to hold hearings and issue subpoenas
Empower the mayor to veto individual elements of the council-passed budget, while allowing the council to override vetos
Require councilmembers’ roles to be their sole employment while serving
Empower the public ethics commission to establish salaries for the mayor and councilmembers
Require that publication of ordinances within 15 days of passage.
This is a lot to take in. The charter was last updated in 1998 under mayor Jerry Brown. In the time since, dissatisfaction with the outcome of those changes has become fairly widespread, with ideological opponents in government routinely agreeing that changes are needed, though often disagreeing on whether that means giving more power to the mayor or the council. Measure EE is the product of years of back-and-forth debate among officials and analysts overseen by Mayor Barbara Lee. Many of these items are controversial, with strong, good-faith arguments in multiple directions, even among socialists.
For instance, many socialists oppose the consolidation of power among mayors, as opposed to diffusing power among councils, which is arguably more representative and less hierarchical. However others may point to the recent tenure of Mayor Mamdani in New York as demonstration that a strong mayoral authority can be a highly effective means to translate public will into a transformative city-wide agenda.
Overall, the proposed changes contain no obvious poison pills designed to strip voters of their power. The most controversial element is the mayoral line-item veto, but taken as a whole most of these changes are positive: for instance, service on Oakland’s city council should not be a side-job, and council members should have no other employment. Additionally, the current restrictions on council members ability to oversee departments on the behalf of their constituents is widely derided across political alignments.
DSA generally favors clear democratic accountability through the legislative branch rather than separately elected executive offices with competing mandates. Similarly, socialists generally oppose giving the chief executive the ability to veto the decisions of the legislative branch.

Prop 1 - Veteran and Income-Qualified Housing Bond Measure
Reviewed Copy link Print race
TL;DR: California DSA recommends a YES vote on Prop 1.
Recommendation Source: California DSA
Prop 1 authorizes $11.25 billion in state bonds, mainly for multifamily affordable housing, preservation and supportive housing, veterans' housing, homeownership assistance, and farmworker, tribal, and student housing.
California's housing crisis is fundamentally a result of housing being treated as a commodity; working-class tenants bear the consequences through enormous rents, displacement, and homelessness. Prop 1 is a flawed solution, but the positives outweigh the negatives. These bonds create a real public subsidy for housing that the private market will not produce at affordable rents. The proposition includes billions for affordable rental construction/preservation and supportive housing. However, as socialists we must note that bonds are a bad way to finance public investment compared with progressive taxation: the state ultimately pays hundreds of millions annually in debt service to bondholders. This is fundamentally a band-aid; we need to tax the rich and build state capacity to build social housing at scale, on the road to decommodifying housing.
Prop 1 is supported by some developer and real estate interests, but also by most affordable housing advocates and organized labor. Prop 2 - Budget Stabilization Account Cap Increase and Gann Limit Changes Amendment
Reviewed Copy link Print race
TL;DR: California DSA recommends a YES vote on Prop 2.
Recommendation Source: California DSA
Prop 2 raises the state reserve cap from 10% to 20%, requires more savings when revenues surge, and importantly excludes deposits into those reserves from California's Gann spending limit.
Recessions routinely become excuses for austerity. Capitalists use economic precarity to justify cuts to schools, health care, public workers, and social programs precisely when working people need them most. Larger reserves give the state greater ability to maintain services during downturns, rather than balancing budgets on workers and service recipients. Excluding reserve deposits from the Gann Limit also slightly weakens one of California's artificial constitutional constraints on public spending.
The California Labor Federation has endorsed Prop 2, as have most progressive institutional endorsers. Opposition comes primarily from Republicans and fiscal conservatives who are hostile to redistributive politics.
Saving money rather than spending it immediately is not intrinsically progressive; socialists generally don't fetishize balanced budgets or reserves for their own sake. However, this proposal leaves workers better off in the long run by providing more security at the state level. A larger cushion against austerity is better than mandated tax rebates/cuts triggered by California's fiscal constitution.
Prop 5 - Eliminate State Officer Recall Successor Elections Amendment
Reviewed Copy link Print race
TL;DR: California DSA recommends a YES vote on Prop 5.
Recommendation Source: California DSA
Prop 5 ends the simultaneous successor contest when a statewide officer is recalled. If the governor is removed, the lieutenant governor succeeds; other vacancies are filled under normal law rather than by a second question on the recall ballot.
This is not a sexy reform, nor is it a socialist one, but it is basic good government housekeeping. California's recall system creates a bizarre democratic loophole in which a majority can elect someone normally, then a smaller and differently composed electorate can remove them and potentially install a replacement with only a small plurality. Previous gubernatorial recalls have had dozens or even hundreds of candidates on the ballot, and often only recall supporters will even vote in the simultaneous successor contest, meaning a tiny plurality of voters can choose the governor.
The recall mechanism should answer the democratic question 'should this official be removed?' rather than create an alternative low-turnout route to power for a minority of voters. Despite its abuse in California, socialists shouldn't oppose recall as a democratic mechanism itself. Recall has long been a democratic demand of historical socialist movements. We should, however, eliminate the anti-majoritarian successor-election mechanism.

Prop 37 creates up to a $25 billion revenue-bond program providing qualifying middle-income buyers a second mortgage worth up to 17% of a newly built home's price. The bonds are repaid from borrowers' mortgage payments rather than the state budget.
Housing unaffordability is a real crisis for workers. This proposition could genuinely help individual working families while simultaneously using state machinery to support private real-estate transactions, developers, and asset ownership. Renters and most working class Californians don’t get much benefit from Prop 37. Prop 37 can deliver some real material benefit to some working class Californians, but it’s not a socialist solution.
Prop 38 - Establish Immunology and Immunotherapy Research Institute Initiative
Reviewed Copy link Print race
TL;DR: California DSA recommends a NO vote on Prop 38.
Recommendation Source: California DSA
Prop 38 borrows $8.4 billion for immunology/immunotherapy research—and directs half the proceeds to one qualifying UC-affiliated research institute run by a billionaire, with the rest distributed through grants.
Public investment in biomedical research is obviously good and something we strongly support. But Prop 38 is not constructed primarily to best facilitate this research. The eligibility language appears effectively tailored to the California Institute for Immunology and Immunotherapy; billionaire Gary Michelson is its co-founder/board chair and the chief financial force behind the initiative. FPPC data show roughly $36M in top support contributions dominated by Michelson/Michelson Center and Meyer Luskin.
This proposition is pretty naked self-dealing by wealthy individuals. By phrasing it as promoting medical research, something no one opposes, Michelson hopes to smuggle in a popular mandate to deliver state funds to his own institution. Voters should not constitutionally commit billions in public debt through an initiative constructed around a particular private institution. Instead, we should democratically fund actual public institutions that are accountable for their work. California must expand publicly governed research, make discoveries broadly available, and fund it through progressive taxation.
Prop 44 - Spending Requirements for Federally Qualified Health Centers Initiative
Reviewed Copy link Print race
TL;DR: California DSA recommends a YES vote on Prop 44.
Recommendation Source: California DSA
Prop 44 requires nonprofit federally qualified health centers (FQHCs) to spend at least 90% of revenue on 'program services' advancing their charitable purpose, intended as patient service delivery and workforce stability, as opposed to management and overhead spending.
Waivers, providing a temporary pause of this requirement, may be requested and issued by the Department of Public Health in exceptional circumstances. These circumstances would have to directly impact the clinic's ability to continue to operate, leading to the closure of patient services or programs and the loss of jobs.
These nonprofits receive enormous amounts of public health care money while operating as private institutions whose management structures are not democratically controlled by workers and patients. The measure attempts to constrain how much revenue can be diverted away from the charitable/health-care mission into management compensation and administrative overhead and related spending.
Socialists believe these should be public institutions, not private institutions funded by the government. We certainly think they should treat their workers well. That is, however, not how things work, and in the near to medium-term future, these institutions will continue to exist.
There is a catch here as well: SEIU-UHW (Service Employees International Union-United Healthcare Workers West) is the sole major financial force behind Yes, putting about $17 million into it. This proposition exists essentially as a tool for SEIU-UHW's organizing strategy more so than an attempt to craft the best possible public policy.
There are reasons for skepticism, as FQHCs serve poor and uninsured patients, and these changes could have unintended consequences: some level of administration, IT, facilities, and other overhead costs can actually be necessary to deliver care. On the other hand, the temporary waivers are intended as a tool to preserve the clinic workforce and access to care for these patients.
However, this is fundamentally a class struggle issue, and the opposition is financed overwhelmingly by the clinic industry: AltaMed, the California Primary Care Association, and numerous individual health-center operators. The existence of currently socially necessary nonprofit employers doesn't mean workers or the public should defer to those employers' claims about how public health-care dollars must be managed.
Prop 45 - Expedited Environmental Review Process for Certain Projects Initiative
Reviewed Copy link Print race
TL;DR: California DSA recommends a NO vote on Prop 45.
Recommendation Source: California DSA
Prop 45 is an initiative statute backed by the California Chamber of Commerce that makes sweeping changes to the California Environmental Quality Act (CEQA). Prop 45 creates a fast-track version of CEQA for a broad range of 'essential' projects, including housing, transportation, energy, water, healthcare, schools, broadband, and public-safety infrastructure.
CEQA can be and is regularly abused to delay housing, transit, clean energy, and other necessary infrastructure at the expense of working class Californians. We don’t default to defending all regulatory and permitting processes just because they give the government power over private business. This is a genuinely thorny issue, with many costs and benefits and bad actors on both sides that can be difficult for socialists to disentangle. CEQA does desperately need further reform. Prop 45 sets fast timelines. It also limits agencies' obligation to study feasible alternatives and restricts the evidence and remedies available to courts reviewing approvals for a broad range of housing and infrastructure projects. Beyond this, it gives much of the housing it fast-tracks weaker labor standards than the other 'essential' projects it covers.
For socialists, the key question we care about here is who gains power when review is weakened? As certified Bad Guy Alec Baldwin says in The Departed, 'cui bono?' A good reform would accelerate democratically planned affordable housing, transit, and green infrastructure, while preserving workers' and affected communities' power over pollution and development. Prop 45 instead applies broad deregulation across many kinds of private and public projects, concentrating this power in the hands of developers themselves. There’s a reason for this. This reform was written by and for some of the most rapacious capitalists in the state.
The Yes campaign is heavily financed by the Building Industry Association, Chamber of Commerce, Edison, Realtors, developers, and billionaire cutout Building a Better California, a veritable rogues gallery of California politics. The No campaign includes most environmental groups and the state building trades. The latter is notable: the building trades will generally support nearly all construction. The old joke is they would pave over the Pacific Ocean if there was a project labor agreement.
California has already undertaken CEQA reform via AB 130 and SB 131, which broadly exempt urban infill housing from CEQA review. Prop 45’s new reforms use the word 'essential' to bring in drastic new changes: covering not only apartments and transit but highways and road expansions, subdivisions, private medical-office buildings, major water systems, power infrastructure, and more. Expanding urban housing and giant infrastructure projects present radically different environmental problems, and need different regulatory regimes. We need more housing and transit; that doesn’t mean gutting regulation on highways and data centers. CEQA has been abused by wealthy homeowners, yes, but it has also been a vital tool for working class communities fighting warehouses, freeway expansions, industrial pollution, contaminated sites, and infrastructure that capitalists and more politically powerful actors want to dump in their communities. The answer is to make it harder for wealthy NIMBYs to block socially useful housing and transit, not to strip the ability of under-resourced working class Californians to fight environmental harm.
If passed, this proposition can only be legislatively amended at all by a 2/3rds vote of the legislature. Any defects or unintended consequences would therefore be incredibly difficult to fix through the ordinary democratic process. California history is littered with examples of poorly worded propositions creating seemingly permanent unintended negative consequences.
CEQA needs reform; California needs a faster process to allow our state to build housing at the needed scale. The housing crunch has been incredibly damaging to working class Californians and their ability to live in this state. CEQA needing further reform doesn’t mean developers and the Chamber of Commerce should be writing that reform. Socialists should support targeted reform, not less reform. This means by-right approval for affordable/social housing, transit, and clean energy. We should support greater public planning capacity; strong labor standards; and targeted limits on bad-faith litigation from wealthy and well-connected homeowners who want to block affordable housing and public transit. But we still need community and environmental protections, as well as a democratic process for development. Prop 45 is not the solution.

Prop 39 - Voter Identification, Citizenship Verification, and Registered Voter List Administration Initiative
Reviewed Copy link Print race
TL;DR: California DSA endorses a NO vote on Prop 39.
Recommendation Source: California DSA
Prop 39 is a Republican initiative that requires government-issued ID for in-person voters and identifying information for mail voting, plus new citizenship-verification/reporting requirements.
Voting is a democratic right, not something conditioned on navigating unnecessary administrative barriers. This is a proposal to add as many barriers as possible to voters to stop a non-existent fraud problem. Reactionary anti-democratic forces want to disenfranchise working class Californians in an effort to rig our elections. People with less money, less stable housing, limited transportation, or complicated documentation are least able to absorb new restrictions. This is just one more step in the fascist MAGA movement’s quest to disenfranchise the poor and anti-democratically rig our elections for their own benefit.
The Yes campaign has raised more than $28 million and is heavily financed by conservative billionaire Richard Uihlein and Carl DeMaio's Reform California. Official opposition includes ACLU, Common Cause, the League of Women Voters, civil rights groups, and most of California’s labor movement. It also includes us.
Prop 41 - Prohibit Excluding New State Taxes from Spending Limit and Require Special Tax Audits Initiative
Reviewed Copy link Print race
TL;DR: California DSA endorses a NO vote on Prop 41.
Recommendation Source: California DSA
Prop 41 is one of two measures put on the ballot by billionaires to thwart the Billionaire Tax (Prop 40). Prop 41 supposedly creates audits of new state tax revenues and makes them subject to a cap. The Billionaire Tax will easily earn more than the cap, over which any money is either returned to voters or goes to education. It doesn’t mention the Billionaire Tax directly, and contains a standard California poison pill that if it receives more votes than other measures with an overlapping topic (the billionaire tax), it nullifies it.
This measure, like 42, is ultimately about defeating the billionaire tax and more generally, the possibility of expanding the public sphere. One of the primary funders of Prop 41 is Sergey Brin, who recently surpassed Jeff Bezos to become the third richest person in the world.
Prop 42 - Prohibit New Taxes on Retirement Holdings, Personal Assets, and Savings and Limit Retroactive Taxes Initiative
Reviewed Copy link Print race
TL;DR: California DSA endorses a NO vote on Prop 42.
Recommendation Source: California DSA
Prop 42 is the second of two measures put on the ballot to thwart the Billionaire Tax (Prop 40). It prohibits new taxes on personal property including retirement accounts, financial assets, or business interests. It deceptively proposes that unnamed 'Sacramento politicians' are planning to do this to everyone. The only tax it would apply to, without naming it, is the Billionaire Tax. Like with 41, more votes for 42 than 40 nullifies the Billionaire Tax. This would very directly block the advancement of the kinds of wealth taxes that can achieve a much needed redistribution of resources and power.
Prop 43 - Two-Thirds Vote Requirement for Local Special Tax Initiatives and Property Tax Initiative Prohibition Amendment
Reviewed Copy link Print race
TL;DR: California DSA endorses a NO vote on Prop 43.
Recommendation Source: California DSA
Prop 43, backed by the Howard Jarvis Taxpayer Association, aims to make it harder to raise local revenue by changing the threshold on local ballot measures that raise taxes from a simple majority to a ⅔ supermajority. This would apply to initiatives placed on a local ballot on or after . This new supermajority vote requirement would apply to measures raising revenue for all types of local governments, including cities, counties, special districts, school and community college districts, and any other local or regional government entity.
Prop 43 would undermine basic principles of democracy by making it easier for big corporate interests like the Realtor’s Association, the Chamber of Commerce, the fossil fuel industry, and other likely suspects to kill funding for the things we need by propagandizing a sufficient minority to prevent funding of public goods.
