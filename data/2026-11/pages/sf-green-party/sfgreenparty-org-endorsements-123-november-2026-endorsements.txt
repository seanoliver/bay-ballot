SF Candidates:
Congress D11 - Connie Chan
The San Francisco Green Party endorses Connie Chan for the CA-11 U.S. House seat. Chan currently serves as the District 1 Supervisor on the SF Board of Supervisors.
Greens were impressed by her responsiveness as a local elected official, her commitment to upholding progressive values, and in particular, her expertise on housing policy, which is a serious issue for San Franciscans. Chan distinguishes herself from her opponent in the race by insisting on sustainable housing that would not lead to further gentrification or de-regulation. She also has strong labor support and a demonstrated history of progressive organizing in San Francisco.
It is unusual for a local Green Party to endorse a Democratic candidate, especially in a partisan race. However, we chose to weigh in in order to prevent the election of a far more dangerous candidate, State Senator Scott Wiener. Wiener has been a tireless lackey for the real estate industry and tech billionaires, as well as the "Israel First" crowd who demand the censorship of any language critical of Israel on our campuses and in our civic institutions.
We recognize Chan's stance on Israel is not ideal, and intend to pressure her to move to a complete arms embargo on Israel rather than her current support of the "Block the Bombs" Act (and continuing to send "defensive" military aid to Israel).
Supervisor, D4 - Jeremy Greco
Jeremy Greco is a SF State graduate who has lived in the Sunset for 26 years and is currently raising a family there. He is known for his time as a worker-owner at the Other Avenues Food Co-op. We previously awarded Greco a ranked endorsement in the June election, and we now award him our sole endorsement in the current contest for D4 Supe.
Greco shares many values with the Greens, including a dedication to grassroots democracy. Unlike the current Supervisor, who has been a rubber stamp for Mayor Lurie, Greco would stand against City Hall corruption and represent his constituents from neighborhood instead of tech interests. His policy stances on issues relevant to our values of ecological wisdom and social justice are also well-aligned with the Green Party's values and platform.
Supervisor, D6 - no endorsement
Neither candidate for D6 Supervisor sought the Green Party's endorsement. The incumbent has been a disaster whose primary contribution to City governance has been expanding the appalling street conditions of the Tenderloin into the Mission. He also spends hours on social media, where with drug-addled logic he equates opponents of the Israeli genocide with antisemites and terrorists. It's unfortunate that the neighborhood residents who are so badly misrepresented by this guy were unable to recruit a serious challenger.
Supervisor, D8 - Michael Nguyen
Michael Nguyen is a longtime advocate for renters and an LGBTQ+ and AAPI community organizer. We are proud to endorse him for D8 Supe.
Unlike the last several Supervisors from his district, Nguyen has good ideas on how to solve the homelessness crisis. He would partially roll back Newsom's disastrous "Care not Cash" initiative, which has resulted in the immiseration of thousands of homeless people while enriching the directors of some politically well-connected non-profits. Nguyen would restore cash assistance to people in need, while also investing in homeless shelters, drug treatment, and permanent housing.
Nguyen also displays a refreshing populist streak, supporting the right of San Franciscans to elect their own representatives in the case of a recall election, rather than maintaining the Mayor's current power to appoint sock puppets who do not represent their constituents who live in the district.
We're pleased to finally see a genuine progressive running in D8, and give Nguyen our sole endorsement.
Supervisor, D10 - Pearci "PJ" Bastiany III
PJ Bastiany is a martial artist and community organizer who is running a campaign in support of affordable housing, safety, and opportunity in District 10. We awarded him our sole endorsement.
Although he's a member of the Democratic Party, Bastiany is a fierce opponent of the Israeli genocide in Gaza. He became a supporter of Green Party gubernatorial candidate Butch Ware due to Ware's outspoken advocacy for Palestinians. Bastiany also shares a number of other Green Values, including strong support for housing that is truly affordable to current members of the community, as well as an emphasis on creative, community-based public safety measures as an alternative to simply throwing more money at the police. He also supports a rollback of "care not cash" to give assistance directly to people in need.
Although he's been excluded from coverage by mainstream press outlets (including Mission Local), we think Bastiany is an exciting candidate and we look forward to hearing more of his ideas in the future. We award him our sole endorsement.
School Board - Virginia Cheung and Alida Fisher
There are three seats up for election on the School Board. The San Francisco Green Party endorses Virginia Cheung and Alida Fisher.
A key concern of Greens in this contest was the issue of school choice. With the lottery-based school assignment system that the Board is rushing to phase out, parents and students are able to choose any schools throughout the City. We fear that a new system that prevents parents from applying to send their kids to schools other than their designated "neighborhood school" will overly restrict parental choice will result in a balkanization of our district. Parents choose schools for many reasons, including the educational specialty of each school (e.g., some specialize in teaching English learners, others may offer immersion in another language), starting/ending times, PTA organization, or even dress code - not just the proximity to their home. If parents are prevented from making these choices, we could see a collapse in support for public schools as parents see tax dollars directed to other schools where their children are no longer allowed to apply.
Greens have endorsed Virginia Cheung in her last two elections, and we hope she can finally break through this time. We continue to be impressed by her commitment to supporting students of all backgrounds, particularly students who speak English as a second language. She is also very supportive of equitable funding, fighting school closures, and is a huge supporter of UESF (and was a strike liaison during their recent strike). Cheung is one of the the UESF-endorsed candidates in this election. Although Cheung supports higher priority for children applying to their neighborhood schools, she understands the need for caution in changing the system, as well as the risks to public education we outline above.
Greens had not previously endorsed incumbent Board member Alida Fischer, but we're endorsing her this time, primarily because she has a good understanding of the risks of moving to an assignment system that cuts parents off from the ability to choose good schools throughout SF. We believe that the current "school assignment crisis" and the rush to reform it into a more segregated system is the first step in converting our public schools into privately-run charters. Of all the incumbents on the Board, Fisher seems to be the most in support of real public education and the one who best understands the pitfalls of moving to an unpopular system that would ban most families in SF from accessing the best public schools that our taxes pay for.
Although we endorsed Laurance Lee in a previous election, we are not endorsing him this time. Lee had been a valuable fiscal watchdog in the past, but has not shown the same inclination to challenge the SFUSD Board in the years since billionaire-backed recall elections allowed Mayor Breed to appoint a pro-privatization Board majority.
College Board - Leah LaCroix and Jeremy Lee
City College continues to face the same challenges as it has faced for decades, with the tech and real estate lobby trying to convert it from an institution that enriches the lives of all San Franciscans into something more similar to a private trade school. City College recently closed their downtown campus, and there is speculation that the campus could be rented to a private university looking for a satellite campus in SF. In contrast, the CCSF faculty union wants to increase the number of classes (especially those that are most popular with San Franciscans) and preserve Free City College for SF residents. They have backed a slate of candidates who oppose privatization. The Green Party is endorsing two of those candidates, Leah LaCroix and Jeremy Lee.
Leah LaCroix gave great answers to our questionnaire, and she has clear ideas for increasing revenue while maintaining Free City College. She has been involved with CCSF for her entire life, as she grew up near City College and took classes there while in high school. LaCroix wants to increase course offerings, specifically for courses with long wait lists that are the most popular with San Franciscans. She also wants to increase transparency and public oversight of the budget. This will have the effect of reducing corruption in contracting, as well as ensure San Franciscans that the college is financially sustainable for the long term.
Jeremy Lee is co-president of the Rose Pak Asian American Club, and is also a staunch defender of Free City College. He generally agrees with LaCroix on issues of transparency. He has a history of activism in the AAPI community, as well as a track record of working with local politicians to campaign for Free City College.
Both of these candidates will be essential to steer the college in the right direction.
SF Ballot Props:
NO on A - commission reform to lower public oversight
Prop A would enact a whole slew of changes to the City's commissions and boards that provide public oversight over City departments. Although proponents claim the measure would streamline efficiency, the net result would be a lot less public oversight and a transfer of power to the Mayor's office.
Greens believe that more oversight and transparency is the best way to reduce the amount of illegal behavior by our notoriously corrupt 1-party government. Vote NO on A to keep the present commission structure.
YES on B - keep moving towards a public bank
Prop B would continue the City's slow progress towards creating a public bank. Greens have advocated for and worked towards a public bank for decades, because public banks save taxpayers a lot of money compared to storing public funds in private banks. Although the initial vision for SF's public bank would only be a lender for construction projects, we hope it will get off the ground and eventually expand operations into a "retail" bank where San Franciscans can deposit money and make loans. This would save ordinary working class San Franciscans a lot of money that currently goes towards private investor profits.
Greens enthusiastically endorse a YES vote on Prop B!
no consensus for or against C - housing trust fund
Prop C would continue to fund the Housing Trust fund, which was established in 2012 by another Prop C.
In 2012, we also took no position on establishing the Housing Trust Fund. Although we strongly support housing that is truly affordable to working class residents of San Francisco (such as the Community Housing Act form of social housing), we were divided on whether or not to support that specific plan or try to work on something better. Our major concern at the time was that the fund would be controlled by the Mayor's office, with inadequate public oversight.
Now, 14 years later, we are at the same impasse as before. Some Greens think that the Trust Fund is doing sufficient good work that it should continue. Others argued that the Mayor is using the funding to keep progressive non-profits on a short leash and thereby limit public organizing for better forms of affordable housing such as tenant-governed social housing for residents of all income levels.
Because we did not reach consensus, we hope Greens will make up your own mind on how to vote on Prop C.
NO on D - restricting ballot access for future propositions
Prop D would quadruple the number of signatures required for the public to put initiatives on the ballot. This would severely limit grassroots democracy in SF, so Greens strongly oppose it.
In 2003, Greens organized and led a citizens' ballot measure to increase the minimum wage in SF and index it to inflation. We gathered most of the signatures using volunteers, saving our limited funds for campaigning in support of the measure. If the signature threshold had been four times higher, we never would have been able to put this measure before voters, because we would not have been able to afford to pay paid contractors to gather the large number of signatures.
If Prop D passes, only wealthy special interests such as the tech and real estate industry will be able to afford to pay to get measures on the ballot. Grassroots activists such as the Greens will have no chance.
Prop D would be the end of grassroots democracy in SF, and would likely lead to a long period of unchallenged oligarchic control of SF politics, so we couldn't more strongly urge people to vote NO!
NO on E - less oversight over City contracting
Prop E would give the Board of Supervisors less oversight over City contracts, preventing them from amending or rejecting the contract unless the cost is over $25 million. Greens think this would lead to more "pay to play" politics in SF, and endorse a NO vote.
Last year, Mayor Lurie awarded a $6 million no-bid contract to OpenGov, a company his family has investments in, and which had given Lurie's Tipping Point nonprofit tens of thousands of dollars in donation. The contract was later amended to add an extension worth an additional $22 million.
Although the current Board of Supervisors appears to have little interest in controlling Lurie's corruption, we should not take away their critical oversight role. Vote NO on Prop E!
NO on F - more unelected, corrupt appointees and less accountability
Like Props D and E, Prop F would also centralize more power in the Mayor's office. It would allow the Mayor to unilaterally fire members of commissions before their terms expired, meaning the Mayor would be able to control these supposedly independent oversight bodies. It would allow the Mayor to appoint unelected "deputy Mayors" who would have no public oversight and would be subject to even more bribery and favors.
Prop F means less public accountability and more corruption. Greens endorse a NO vote.
no position for or against G - the measure to open the Great Highway to cars on weekdays
Prop G would re-open the Great Highway to cars on weekdays, overturning 2024's Prop K, which converted the highway into a park. Although Greens opposed the original Prop K, we reached consensus in taking no position on overturning it.
Greens continue to have concerns about the environmental effects of our "WreckInPark" department hosting large revenue-generating activities on top of environmentally sensitive dunes. And we also expect the park to intensify real estate development and contribute to the gentrification of the neighborhood. There has never been a formal environmental review of the project, which moved traffic into other environmentally sensitive areas.
However, we are not supportive of the process of continuing to re-litigate issues through ballot measures, especially when the original vote demonstrated high Citywide support for the park. And at this point, a more environmental option (such as a park run by the GGNRA) is probably not possible.
There are more important things on the ballot (especially voting down Prop D). Spending energy on Prop G instead would not be in the neighborhood's (or San Francisco's) interest.
YES on H - progressive parcel tax to fund Muni
Prop H is a progressive parcel tax to fund Muni. Greens have opposed sales taxes to fund transit in the past, and think that transit should be entirely funded through progressive taxation (i.e., free to riders). Prop H is a large step in the right direction, and we strongly support it.
Prop H is about as progressive a tax as San Francisco can enact (since we aren't allowed to collect an income tax). Larger homes and businesses would pay a lot more under Prop H than smaller homes and apartments. And Muni is critically important; Prop H will be a lifeline if the less popular Prop RTM fails.
We have some concerns about tenant pass-throughs in Prop H, which even at the low end of the scale ($65 per unit per year) will create a considerable burden on our lowest income renters. But Prop H might be a harder sell to voters without any pass-through, so we can live with this. Vote YES on Prop H.
no consensus for or against I - dedicate $120 million from real estate transfer tax to "affordable housing"
Prop I would make changes to the real estate transfer tax (2020's Prop I), which we strongly supported. The original Prop I brought in $200 million per year by taxing the sale of very expensive properties (those worth over $10 million). This revenue has since increased to $240 million/year, and is an excellent means of taxing the rich. Although Greens strongly support the current transfer tax, we did not reach consensus on the new Prop I, which would redirect about half of the money ($120 million/year) to affordable housing run by nonprofits. We therefore did not take a position supporting or opposing Prop I.
Prop I starts with a mostly revenue-neutral tweak that would raise taxes on the highest value properties while also exempting new development (another example of the control of real estate developers over City politics). But Prop I's major effect would be to require that $120 million/year would be required to be spent on affordable housing run by nonprofits. Some Greens think that such projects are a good use of money and essential to preventing homelessness and displacement. Other Greens argued that the money should be invested in a better affordable housing model instead (such as tenant-governed social housing for people of all income levels), as the non-profit housing model results in poorly run developments and is subject to corruption. Since we did not reach consensus, we hope that individual Greens will weigh both sides and make up your own mind.
YES on J - end exemption to real estate transfer tax by banks foreclosing on properties
Prop J is another tweak to the real estate transfer tax, discussed in Prop I, above. Currently, banks that foreclose on properties do not pay this tax, as it does not count as a "transfer." Prop J would end this exemption, and tax banks that foreclose on buildings. This is important for two reasons. First, big property investment companies can drop rental properties on banks when the income goes down, then form a new LLC and buy them back from the bank at a substantially reduced value to reduce both mortgage amount and property taxes as the "value" of the property substantially diminished, while rental income remains unchanged. Additionally, foreclosures are one of the ways we wind up with homelessness in the Bay Area and this would make banks less inclined to foreclose and force marginal income families out of their homes.
Greens endorsed YES on J, as it will both raise money and reduce evictions.
no consensus for or against RTM - regional sales taxes to fund BART, Muni, and other local transit
RTM is a regional transit measure, which would fund Bay Area transit (including BART and local systems like AC Transit and Muni) through sales taxes. The tax would be 1% in SF, and 0.5% elsewhere in the Bay Area. Greens did not reach consensus on RTM, so we did not take a position for or against the measure.
As discussed above in our Prop H endorsement, Greens support funding transit through progressive taxation, not regressive measures such as sales taxes. In the past, the state of CA paid for a lot more of the costs of public transit than it does now. So over time, the Democrats running our state have shifted the cost of transit from progressive taxes on rich people and corporations onto our lowest income residents.
When Prop RTM was being drafted, activists proposed a more popular, progressive tax on large Bay Area corporations instead of a sales tax. That was blocked by local politicians including RTM's co-author Scott Wiener, who wanted to tax poor people instead of his donor base.
Greens did not reach consensus on whether or not to support RTM. Some Greens argued that the Dems are playing "chicken" by threatening to drastically scale back critical systems like BART and Muni, and will come up with other state funding if voters reject an unpopular sales tax. However, other Greens argued that the risk of actual cuts to critical systems would be too great. We urge individual Greens to make up your own mind and vote accordingly.
CA Ballot Props:
NO on 1 - bond to fund private housing for veterans
Prop 1 is an $11 billion bond to that funds a number of housing-related things, from rehabilitating affordable housing to helping veterans purchase private homes. It is similar to 2018's Prop 1 and 2008's Prop 12, both of which we also opposed. As we said at the time:
While we strongly support our veterans, and would support giving veterans a higher priority in getting into publicly financed housing, we don't think that such a large sum of public money should be issued exclusively on private housing for veterans, while ignoring other people in more desperate need for housing.
When bonds are used, they should be used only for urgent investment in the public sector. Public bond money should not be used for private cars or private housing.
The same arguments still apply, so we urge a NO vote on Prop 1. Also, please refer to our Statement on Bond Funding, below.
NO on 2 - put more state tax money in "rainy day fund" instead of priorities like public transit
Prop 2 is also a repeat of a previous bad idea, Prop 2 from 2014. The previous measure changed the maximum size of California's "rainy day fund" from 5% of annual revenue to 10%, and the new Prop 2 would raise that limit again to 20%. This was a bad idea then and a worse idea now. As we wrote at the time:
Unfortunately, our state schools and universities are in a "rainy day" right now. It is foolish to withhold money rather then spending it at a time when spending is desperately needed to stimulate the economy. The tech sector is doing great, but for most other people, the economy is as bad as it's been since the Wall Street meltdown.
We should be spending state money on priorities like public transit rather than raising taxes on poor people. Vote NO on Prop 2.
YES on 3 - keep the tax on rich people to fund public education
Continuing with the string of "greatest hits" ballot measures, Prop 3 is a repeat of 2016's Prop 55, which extended taxes on the top 2% highest-income Californians to pay for public education. Prop 55 was itself a renewal of the best parts of Prop 30 from 2012. Greens strongly supported both previous ballot props. We also enthusiastically endorse Prop 3, which would extend this tax indefinitely instead of letting it expire in 2030.
YES on 4 - allow public financing of candidates statewide
Prop 4 would repeal the ban on public financing of candidates for state office, and for local office in smaller cities throughout the state. Greens strongly endorse this. In 2000, Greens worked on Prop O, which established public financing for SF Board of Supervisors elections, and in 2005 Green Supervisor Ross Mirkarimi championed legislation to extend "voter owned elections" to other SF citywide contests. Public financing is really the only defense that grassroots candidates have to even partially level the playing field against well-funded corporate candidates and their Super PACs. Prop 4 is just a first step towards this; the state and other municipalities would still have to enact local legislation to enact and fund a program. Vote YES on 4 to help make elections a little less corrupt.
NO on 5 - neuter state recall elections instead of reforming them using RCV
Prop 5 would change the way statewide recall elections work: instead of voters choosing a replacement for the recalled elected official in the same election, the seat would just be vacant until the next election. If the Governor were to be recalled, the Lt. Gov would automatically become Governor. Greens oppose Prop 5, as it would completely neuter the power that voters currently have to recall elected officials.
Greens support reforming recall elections to give voters more power. The current system is not very democratic, because candidates in the recall election do not have to win a majority of votes, and normal campaign financing rules don't apply. Because there are often many candidates running, a wealthy candidate could win with a relatively small percentage of the vote, as long as they get more votes than the other candidates. Greens support reforming recall elections to enable ranked choice voting, which would ensure the winning candidate has majority support. We would also support campaign finance regulations to fix the funding problems with recall elections. But Prop 5 would be a step in the wrong direction: it gives voters less power over who represents them rather than more.
Vote NO on 5 to keep recall elections meaningful, and let's work together to demand legislators pass reforms to enable more grassroots democracy rather than less.
NO on 37 - $25 billion bond for subsidized mortgages for new housing
Prop 37 is a $25 billion bond to subsidize mortgages on newly built housing. Greens oppose it. Prop 37 is a taxpayer subsidy to the real estate industry, and a totally inappropriate use of bond money. Please see our Statement on Bond Funding, below.
NO on 38 - $8.4 billion bond for immunology research
Prop 38 is a $8.4 billion bond for immunology research. Greens think this is an inappropriate use of bond money, and oppose it. Although we support publicly funded medical research, if California wants to do this, it should be funded as part of our normal state budget on an annual basis. This is how countries with economies of comparable size to California fund medical research, instead of bonds, which are a giveaway to the wealthy (see our Statement on Bond Funding, below).
We oppose cuts and restrictions on medical research implemented by the federal government, but just like Prop 14 in 2020 and prop 71 in 2004, we see Prop 38 as a performative reaction by Democrats running our state to the actions of Republicans in Congress.
Prop 38 also contains a kickback scheme. The billionaire co-founder of the California Institute for Immunology and Immunotherapy, Gary K. Michelson, is one of the two ultrawealthy chief backers of Proposition 38. He's personally given over $50 million to pass it, and it's likely that his institute would be the only center qualified to receive over $4 billion in funding from Prop 38 (https://calmatters.org/politics/elections/2026/07/proposition-38-california/).
Vote NO on 38 and fund research through taxes rather than bonds.
NO on 39 - make it harder to vote by requiring people to show ID or write it on mail-in ballot
Prop 39 would make it harder to vote by requiring you to show ID at the polls or remember to write in an ID number on your mail-in ballot. Greens strongly oppose this. There is almost no voter fraud in the United States by normal voters - the real crimes are billionaires buying our elections, and Democrats using shady legal tricks to prevent Green candidates from running for office. The problem is not enough people engaged in the political process, not that there are too many people voting. It should be easier to vote and to engage in other aspects of democracy, like running for office and making informed electoral choices. Say NO to Prop 39.
YES on 40 - billionaire wealth tax
Prop 40 would impose a one time tax of 5% on Californians with more than $1 billion in wealth. There are currently around 200 billionaires in CA, who hoard $2 trillion in assets between them. These billionaires have used their wealth to rig our political and economic system in order to elect candidates to office who have lowered their taxes and cater to their every whim. We strongly support Prop 40 to make them pay their fair share for the benefits they receive from CA.
Prop 40 would raise about $100 billion to replace lost federal dollars that have been cut in California at the same time that taxes on billionaires have been reduced. The money would restore cuts to Medicare, public education, and food assistance programs.
Vote YES on 40, and be sure to vote NO on Props 41 and 42, the other ballot measures billionaires have put on the ballot to block it (see below).
NO on 41 - makes it harder to raise taxes, and blocks billionaire tax
Billionaires reacted to Prop 40 by placing Prop 41 (and 42, see below) on the ballot to block it. Prop 41 would require audits of new tax programs and supposedly "eliminate waste" by making it harder to raise taxes in CA. But it also has a poison pill that, if Prop 41 gets more votes than Prop 40, would block the billionaire tax. So it's essential to vote NO on 41 (and 42, see below) at the same time you vote YES on 40.
NO on 42 - blocks billionaire tax
Prop 42 would block any taxes in CA on personal property, including Prop 40 (the billionaire tax). It was placed on the ballot by billionaires, along with Prop 41, in order to block Prop 40. So vote NO on 42 as well as 41.
NO on 43 - makes it harder to raise local taxes (2/3 requirement instead of majority)
Prop 43 would make it harder to raise local taxes. Currently, local ballot measures put on by gather signatures need majority support to raise taxes. Prop 43 would raise that threshold to 2/3. This would be anti-democratic and favor the "no taxes" conservatives over people trying to fund essential services like Muni (see our argument for YES on H). Vote NO on 43 to stop local service cuts in the future.
YES on 44 - SEIU-UHW measure to require more of health clinic revenues to go to services instead of executive pay
This is SEUI-UHW measure to require 90% of health clinic revenues to go to patient services instead of executive pay. We have no idea why this was on the ballot, but suspect it is the latest battle in their ongoing labor struggle with private dialysis clinics. Greens will back the union by voting YES.
NO on 45 - guts environmental protection laws in order to enable data centers and other development
Prop 45 was put on the ballot by real estate developers in order to gut the environmental review process in CA, allowing all kinds of destructive projects (data centers, the Oakland coal terminal, massive YIMBY condo towers) to proceed without regard for long term consequences. These developers want to skip environmental and citizen review to speed up the building process because they know when the problems are revealed the State, or the investment companies that are currently holding the property, will be the ones to face the lawsuits. This is so obvious that even the CA Democratic Party, firmly under the control of big developers, is opposed to Prop 45. Also noted is that the CA Republican Party is for it, just another of their 'poison the well' tactics.
Prop 45 is antithetical to our Key Values of Ecological Wisdom and Social Justice, so vote NO!
SF Green Party Statement on Bond Funding
The SF Green Party has often been hesitant to embrace bond financing. In addition to being environmentally and socially responsible, we are also fiscally responsible. Bond funding requires payments totaling about twice the actual cost of whatever improvements are made, and passes costs on to future generations. Because people who buy bonds are almost exclusively the wealthy, as investors are paid back over the 20-30 year life of the bond, wealth is transferred from middle and low income taxpayers to rich bondholders.
Bond funding also helps rich people avoid paying their fair share of taxes, since interest on municipal bonds is exempt from both state and federal tax. As noted in the California Voter Guide in 1992, over 35,000 U.S. millionaires supplemented their income with tax exempt state and local bond checks averaging over $2,500 per week (that's over $130,000 per year tax free). They avoided paying federal and state taxes on over $5 billion, which must be made up by the rest of us. The SF Green Party calls on the public to join us in working to phase out this regressive and unfair subsidy of the rich and their investment bankers (who take millions of dollars off the top when the bonds are issued).
There are a few cases in which Greens have supported bond measures. In general, we are willing to support bonds that are issued to in order to build urgently needed, publicly-owned infrastructure, such as a public hospital or high speed rail. We generally oppose bonds that fund ongoing maintenance projects; these should be paid for using City revenues (which should be increased by raising taxes on the wealthy).
