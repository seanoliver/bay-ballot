Editorial: Pittsburg Must Look at the Big Picture, Not a Pixel
By Mike Burkholder
by CC News
written by CC News
[Pittsburg]
87
Pittsburg has an opportunity to do something very special over the next few years. But to capitalize on it, residents — and the next City Council — have to stop staring at one pixel and start looking at the entire picture.
Yes, I’m talking about data centers.
I get it. Data centers are scary to some people. Few residents are going to throw a block party celebrating one being proposed near their neighborhood. There are legitimate questions about energy, water, noise, land use and whether they fit the long-term vision of a community.
Those conversations should happen.
But what’s done is done. Lessons have been learned. Policy can be improved.
What we cannot do is allow one issue to consume an entire city.
Unfortunately, the data center debate has also brought out some of the worst behavior in local politics: name-calling, intimidation, threats, campaign rhetoric and, in some cases, claims that simply are not true.
People are allowed to be angry. They are allowed to disagree. They are allowed to demand better.
But cooler heads eventually have to prevail.
And this narrative that residents were completely ignored? That’s not accurate. In many cases, residents simply weren’t paying attention years ago when decisions were being made. That happens in local government all the time.
You cannot retroactively turn a lack of public participation into a conspiracy.
More importantly, complaining about what happened is not a governing philosophy. If you want a seat on the City Council, tell voters what you intend to do about Pittsburg’s next 10 years — not just what you’re angry about from the last two.
So here we are: 11 candidates fighting for three seats.
Good luck sorting that out.
FULL ENDORSEMENTS
Mayor Dionne Adams is the one candidate in this field who should be on every resident’s ballot.
Adams understands Pittsburg. She has advocated for greater community outreach and recognizes that when it comes to economic development, Pittsburg cannot afford to sit around waiting for opportunities to magically appear.
On the data center issue, Adams has taken plenty of criticism. Some of it comes with the territory. Some of it has been unfair. She has explained her votes and the timeline behind them, and her explanation makes sense.
She is also pushing for a pause on future data centers while the city develops better policy — exactly what government should do when circumstances change: learn, adjust and move forward.
More importantly, Pittsburg needs people who understand that the city’s future extends far beyond one development project. Adams’ relationships and understanding of regional and state government will matter as Pittsburg fights for investment, infrastructure and economic opportunities.
She gets my vote.
Wolfgang Croskey deserves an opportunity to serve Pittsburg from the dais.
From a budget and economic development standpoint, he could bring something the council desperately needs: someone willing to aggressively advocate for the business community while questioning whether City Hall is doing things simply because that’s how they’ve always been done.
Pittsburg needs someone willing to think outside the box.
Will Wolf Croskey occasionally drive city staff bonkers?
Probably.
Good.
Sometimes government needs someone asking why something takes six months instead of six weeks, why businesses are getting bogged down, or why an economic development opportunity isn’t moving faster.
Croskey would bring a different perspective, and Pittsburg could benefit from it.
COIN FLIP: KOBATA OR JOHNSON
This is where it gets difficult.
For the third seat, Arlene Kobata (Siu Ling) and Pauline Johnson are nearly interchangeable for me — but for very different reasons.
Arlene Kobata may be one of the more underrated members of the council.
She understands how government actually works, has experience, such as serving on the planning commission, that the other candidates simply don’t have. She also understands that governing requires more than showing up angry about the issue of the month.
She also happens to be Pittsburg’s self-proclaimed community cheerleader. I don’t exactly envision her waking up one morning plotting how to sabotage Pittsburg.
Like Adams, Kobata has taken a beating over the data center issue. Fair criticism is part of holding office. Some of what has been thrown her way has gone well beyond that.
If nothing else, she has developed thicker skin.
She’ll need it.
The next four years could include some enormous decisions involving development, infrastructure, the budget and Pittsburg’s economic future. Experience has value.
Of the newcomers, Pauline Johnson may have the most upside.
She brings a fresh perspective without appearing to believe that everything City Hall has ever done is automatically wrong.
That’s important.
Johnson understands local government, understands the controversy surrounding the data center and appears capable of thinking through the unintended consequences of policy rather than simply reacting to whatever issue generates the loudest Facebook thread.
She’s new to the political world, and there will obviously be a learning curve. But she could become an asset to Pittsburg. She’s worth taking a chance on.
SPOILER: CC CARDINALLI-MACALUSO
I absolutely love CC Cardinalli-Macaluso’s social media game and her ability to communicate with residents. She comes across well, she’s engaging and she’s willing to admit she wasn’t paying close attention to some of the decisions that brought Pittsburg to this point.
There’s something refreshing about simply admitting that.
She may not be my pick this time around, but watch out. With more experience, more involvement and a deeper understanding of city operations, she could become a serious player in Pittsburg politics.
THE DART BOARD
Then there is the rest of the field:
Amhari Perkins
Mark Linde
Sonja Shephard
Carolynne Born
Alvin Nelson
Iztaccuauhtli Hector Gonzalez
Maybe one of them surprises everyone.
But with three seats available and several candidates who already offer experience, economic-development knowledge or significant upside, I’m not throwing darts when there are better-defined choices on the ballot.
PITTSBURG NEEDS MORE THAN ONE-ISSUE COUNCILMEMBERS
This election cannot simply become a referendum on data centers.
Pittsburg has bigger issues coming.
The city needs to attract businesses, expand its tax base, create jobs and make it easier — not harder — for responsible investment to occur. It needs to improve its economic-development strategy and become more aggressive about competing with neighboring cities for opportunities.
And then there’s the budget.
Eventually, every political promise runs into a calculator.
The next council will have to make difficult decisions about spending, revenues, staffing, services and priorities. Candidates promising everything to everyone while opposing every project that generates revenue are eventually going to discover that municipal budgets don’t run on applause.
That’s why voters should be looking for councilmembers capable of seeing the whole board.
Ask questions about the data center. Demand better policy. Demand transparency. Learn from what happened.
But then move forward.
Pittsburg cannot spend the next four years fighting the last battle while neighboring cities fight for the next investment.
The city needs leaders who can walk and chew gum at the same time — people who can protect neighborhoods and welcome responsible development; question City Hall and understand how government actually works; control spending and grow the economy.
Because the biggest danger facing Pittsburg isn’t one data center.
It’s becoming so obsessed with one pixel that the city completely misses the big picture.
