RTM [Yes on the Regional Transit Measure]
Regional Transit Measure (RTM)
YES on the Regional Transit Measure
Vote YES to protect transit and avoid longer commutes
This is probably the most important local measure on our ballot. The Bay Area needs public transit to get workers to jobs, students to school, and people across the region to the places they need to go. If our public transit system fails, it will affect everyone: workers can’t get to their employment, traffic worsens, and pollution rises.
Severe service cuts would be catastrophic. For example, BART would have to cut service and close up to 15 stations. Bay Bridge delays are projected to double and add at least 33–43 minutes to commutes. Displaced transit riders would have to pay $3,280 more every year for transportation. More driving and traffic would generate an additional 184,000 metric tons of carbon dioxide each year, undermining climate goals. And these are conservative estimates!
This measure would add a half-cent sales tax in Alameda County for 14 years. The revenue would preserve existing transit service, support local transit and eligible transportation improvements, and fund specific rider benefits. Independent oversight will monitor whether funds are spent as required.
The Bay Area cannot afford to let transit collapse. Vote YES on RTM for our economy, our environment, and a region that remains connected.

U&V [Yes on Measures U and V]
Measures U & V
YES on Measures U and V
Repair what we rely on, and protect the services we count on
YESMeasure U
Repair Berkeley’s aging infrastructure now to avoid costly fixes later
Measure U would repair and improve Berkeley’s aging infrastructure, including fire stations, emergency response facilities, sidewalks, parks, community centers, and public buildings. Berkeley has $1.65–2 billion in unfunded infrastructure needs and deferred maintenance, and the proposed $300 million bond would fund critical infrastructure improvements. An extensive six-month public engagement process led to the proposed project list, which includes rebuilding the Frances Albrier Community Center, renovating King Pool, improving the Adeline Corridor, upgrading parks and waterfront facilities, modernizing fire stations and emergency response facilities, improving the animal shelter, repairing sidewalks, expanding accessibility for people with disabilities, and making seismic improvements to Civic Center buildings.
Measure U is the second iteration of the previous infrastructure bond, Measure T1, which has successfully revitalized Berkeley’s parks, community centers, playgrounds, roadways, and sidewalks. T1 has achieved 100% compliance on biennial audits, but is fully allocated. Berkeley now needs a new funding source to continue addressing its growing infrastructure backlog. Berkeley’s nonpartisan auditor, who has studied the city’s infrastructure maintenance needs, supports Measure U.
Doing nothing will not make these problems disappear. Berkeley does not have sufficient funding to address its existing infrastructure needs, and delaying repairs will only make them more expensive. Measure U offers an opportunity to invest in our community now rather than leave future generations with deteriorating facilities and an even larger bill.
Vote Yes on Measure U to repair what we rely on every day, before the costs multiply.
YESMeasure V
A half-cent sales tax to maintain and protect essential city services and programs.
Measure V keeps core city services funded for Berkeley residents by aligning Berkeley’s sales tax with neighboring Albany and Oakland. Berkeley has already made really difficult choices to address its structural deficit by significantly cutting spending and eliminating positions. Without the passage of Measure V, City Council will have to cut more positions.
These aren’t idle threats. The City identified 33 positions tied to Measure V, including nine fire department positions needed to keep Fire Station 4 on Marin Avenue open, six 911 dispatchers, 15 police officers, and three parks and recreation positions who staff after-school programs, camps, pools, and other community center services.
Measure V adds a modest half-cent sales tax to prevent these cuts and protect public safety, youth, and vital community services. It doesn’t apply to groceries and prescription medications.
Vote Yes on Measure V for a safe, vibrant Berkeley and to prevent cuts to public safety, youth programs, and other essential community services.

Administrative Charter Amendments
YES on Measure W
Minor amendments to comply with state law
Measure W is a noncontroversial measure that makes administrative changes to the charter. It would also align several local election deadlines and procedures with state law.
X [Yes on Measure X]
Rent Stabilization Ordinance Amendments
YES on Measure X
Modest updates to the Rent Stabilization Ordinance
Measure X allows the Rent Board to reduce or waive registration fees for nonprofit affordable-housing providers and allows the City flexibility to set per-unit fees for affordable housing, fixing an unintended consequence of a 2024 amendment. In addition to this change, Measure X also caps annual rent increases at 10%, including previously “banked” increases and ensures that tenants keep the protections they had at the start of their tenancy. The measure strengthens tenant-association rights in 5–9 unit buildings and simplifies certain landlord registration requirements.
Broadly, Measure X closes loopholes in the current Rent Stabilization Ordinance, improves its administration, and provides modest additional tenant protections. Arguments about rent stabilization are a separate debate. Measure X asks voters to approve these targeted changes to Berkeley’s existing law. Vote YES on Measure X.
YesOnXBerkeley.org →
Y [Yes on Measure Y]
Parcel Tax to Fund Arts
YES on Measure Y
Help stabilize the economic future of our local arts and cultural sector
Measure Y would enact a $0.07-per-square-foot parcel tax on property improvements for 12 years raising more than $6 million annually for an Arts Vitality Fund. The fund would support performing and visual arts organizations, individual artists, capital repairs, and community festivals. 75% is directly targeted toward live performing arts. The measure includes exemptions for low-income seniors, very low-income households, and regulated affordable housing.
Beyond direct artistic funding, Measure Y supports Berkeley’s creative economy and guarantees community benefits like free performances, after-school youth programming, and ticket discounts for residents and workers. Civic Arts Commission oversight, public review panels, and annual audits provide accountability and transparency.
Vote YES on Measure Y to protect Berkeley’s vibrant creative economy, support local working artists, and maintain our city as a premier cultural destination for future generations.
YesForBerkeleyArts.com →
Z [No on Measure Z]
Parcel Tax to Fund a Regional Bank
NO on Measure Z
$58 Million from Berkeley for a regional bank that isn’t ready to operate
Measure Z would create a six-year parcel tax expected to raise $58 million from Berkeley property owners to help capitalize and support a regional public bank. However, the bank is not yet authorized to operate because California law requires state approval and FDIC insurance. Measure Z acknowledges that the bank may not receive authorization. If that happens, the measure’s backup plan allowing the funding to make direct loans to affordable housing, green energy, and small businesses would not take effect until .
Still, implementation questions remain unanswered. The city manager must administer and enforce the tax, process exemptions, and prepare annual reports. At the same time, the city auditor must audit it, but the measure doesn’t establish a dedicated staffing plan or funding for this work, and the proponents didn’t consult with them. At a time when the General Fund is under significant pressure, the City could end up absorbing the costs of administering a bank that may never materialize.
Although this has always been envisioned as a regional bank, Berkeley would currently be providing by far the largest identified public investment. The original Public Bank East Bay plan assumed $40 million in pledged deposits from Berkeley, Oakland, Richmond, and Alameda together. Measure Z would instead raise $58.3 million from Berkeley alone. Richmond has pledged $750,000, contingent on approvals and Alameda County participation, but no other commitment has been identified from Oakland or Alameda County. These unresolved questions about authorization, regional cost-sharing, local benefits, and city staffing are why we endorse a NO on Z.
AA [Yes on Measure AA]
Sugar-Sweetened Beverage Distribution Tax
YES on Measure AA
Continue Berkeley’s wildly successful “Big Soda” tax.
Berkeley made history in 2014 as the first U.S. city to adopt a tax on distributors of sugar-sweetened beverages. Over the past decade, the tax has funded school gardens, nutrition education, health screenings, and community programs benefiting children and families throughout Berkeley.
The results are significant. Sugary drink consumption has declined substantially, nearly 80,000 health and dental screenings have been provided, and thousands of students have benefited from school gardening and nutrition programs.
Measure AA would increase Berkeley’s sugary drink distributor tax from one cent to two cents per ounce. The revenue would fund school gardens, nutrition education, clean water access, and community health programs. Measure AA ensures independent oversight to ensure funding supports these specific public health purposes.
Berkeley has ten years of results showing what this tax can do. Vote YES on AA to keep these programs going and bring their benefits to more people.
BerkeleyVsBigSoda.com →
